North Carolina law requires landscape contractors to hold a valid license issued by the NC Landscape Contractors' Registration Board (NCLCRB) before performing regulated landscape contracting work for compensation. Despite this requirement, unlicensed contractors continue to operate in the marketplace — sometimes unknowingly, sometimes not. Whether you are a property owner hiring someone or a contractor evaluating your own compliance, understanding the difference between licensed and unlicensed landscape contractors in NC is essential. This page is an independent informational resource and is not affiliated with the NCLCRB or any government agency.

## Why the Distinction Matters

The licensing requirement under Chapter 89D of the NC General Statutes exists for a reason: landscape contracting involves significant work that can have lasting effects on a property. Grading that is done incorrectly can redirect stormwater toward a home's foundation. Retaining walls that fail can cause property damage and injury. Irrigation systems that are improperly installed can contaminate water supplies or create chronic drainage problems. Trees planted in the wrong location or with improper technique may die or become hazardous.

Licensing ensures that contractors have demonstrated a baseline of competence through examination, possess the qualifying experience the board requires, and are accountable to a regulatory body if problems arise. When you hire a licensed contractor, you have access to a formal complaint and discipline process that simply does not exist with an unlicensed operator. For contractors, operating without a license — even unknowingly — creates significant legal and financial exposure. The distinction is not merely bureaucratic; it is a meaningful difference in accountability, competence verification, and consumer protection.

## What a Licensed Contractor Has Done

A landscape contractor who holds a valid license from the NCLCRB has cleared several significant hurdles before being permitted to work in the regulated scope:

**Passed a written examination.** The NCLCRB administers an examination covering landscape contracting principles, horticultural knowledge, safety, and applicable NC law. Passing this exam demonstrates that the licensee has a measurable level of technical knowledge in the field.

**Met experience requirements.** Applicants must document qualifying experience in landscape contracting before they are eligible to sit for the examination. This means a licensed contractor has real-world background in the field, not just theoretical knowledge.

**Maintained required insurance.** Licensed contractors are required to carry general liability insurance at levels set by the board. This protects property owners in the event that the contractor's work causes damage, and it protects the contractor from uninsured liability.

**Remained accountable to the NCLCRB.** A licensee is subject to the board's rules of professional conduct, continuing education requirements, and disciplinary authority. If a licensed contractor performs substandard work or violates the law, the board can investigate, sanction, suspend, or revoke their license — consequences that create a meaningful incentive for professional behavior.

**Renewed their license on schedule.** Maintaining a valid license requires ongoing compliance with renewal requirements, including completing continuing education units. A licensed contractor is not simply someone who passed a test years ago and has done nothing since.

## Risks of Hiring an Unlicensed Contractor

Hiring an unlicensed landscape contractor for regulated work carries real risks that property owners should understand before signing a contract or handing over a deposit.

**No verified competency.** An unlicensed contractor has not passed the required examination and may not have the knowledge or experience to perform complex landscape work correctly. There is no baseline assurance of technical competence.

**No insurance verification.** The board does not track or enforce insurance requirements for unlicensed operators. Without insurance, if the contractor's work damages your property or injures someone, you may have no recourse against the contractor's insurer — and recovering damages may require lengthy and expensive civil litigation.

**No regulatory accountability.** The NCLCRB's complaint and discipline process only applies to licensed contractors. If you hire an unlicensed contractor and problems arise, the board cannot help you. You have no recourse through the licensing system — your only options are civil court or, in cases involving fraud, criminal complaint to law enforcement.

**Possible contract voidability.** Contracts with unlicensed contractors for regulated work may be legally problematic. In some circumstances, a contract for services that require a license can be challenged as unenforceable. This can leave a property owner who has already paid for work in a difficult legal position, potentially unable to sue for damages under the contract.

**Financial risk.** Unlicensed operators who cause damage and have no insurance or business assets leave property owners bearing the cost of repairs. In the worst cases, a homeowner may pay for landscape work that is done incorrectly and then pay again to have it properly redone by a licensed contractor.

## How to Tell If a Contractor Is Licensed

The most reliable way to verify a landscape contractor's license status is to check directly with the NCLCRB. The board maintains records of licensed contractors and can confirm whether a given individual or business holds a current, valid license. Before committing to a contract:

- **Ask the contractor for their license number.** A licensed contractor should be able to provide their license number without hesitation.
- **Verify the license with the NCLCRB.** Contact the board directly or use any online license verification tool the board makes available to confirm that the license number is valid, active, and not under suspension or other restriction.
- **Look for the license number on bids and contracts.** Licensed contractors often include their license number on written estimates, contracts, and business cards as a matter of professional practice.
- **Don't rely solely on verbal assurances.** A contractor claiming to be licensed is not the same as a contractor being licensed. Always verify independently.

If a contractor is unwilling to provide a license number or becomes defensive when asked, treat that as a significant warning sign.

## What About Subcontractors

Large landscape projects sometimes involve subcontractors — companies or individuals hired by the prime contractor to perform specific portions of the work. In North Carolina, the use of subcontractors does not eliminate the licensing obligation. The prime contractor is responsible for ensuring that regulated landscape contracting work performed on their projects is done by licensed individuals or entities.

If you are a prime contractor using subcontractors for regulated landscape work, you should verify that those subcontractors are properly licensed before engaging them. Using an unlicensed subcontractor for work that requires a license can create liability for the prime contractor, including potential disciplinary action by the NCLCRB and exposure to civil claims. Property owners hiring prime contractors should also understand that a problem caused by an unlicensed subcontractor may still be traceable back to the prime contractor's responsibility.

## Can Unlicensed Contractors Do Any Landscape Work

Yes. Chapter 89D does not regulate all outdoor or landscape-related work. There are categories of work that do not require a landscape contractor license in North Carolina:

- **Routine lawn care and maintenance** — mowing, edging, leaf blowing, and basic yard cleanup are generally exempt from the licensing requirement.
- **Basic pruning and trimming** — routine trimming of shrubs and hedges that does not involve large-scale installation or removal generally falls outside the regulated scope.
- **Employees of licensed contractors** — individuals performing regulated work as employees under the direct supervision of a licensed contractor, where the licensed contractor holds the contract and is responsible for the work, may not themselves need to be independently licensed (though the contractor of record must be).
- **Certain agricultural operations** — work performed as part of normal farming or nursery operations may be exempt under specific circumstances.

The exemptions have limits, however, and the boundary between exempt maintenance work and regulated installation work is not always obvious. When a contractor's services span both exempt and regulated activities, they cannot use the exempt portion of their work to shield the regulated portion from licensing requirements. When in doubt about whether a specific activity is exempt, contact the NCLCRB.

## Legal Consequences for Unlicensed Contractors

Performing regulated landscape contracting work without a valid license is a violation of NC law under Chapter 89D. The consequences for unlicensed practice can include:

**Civil penalties.** The NCLCRB has authority to seek civil penalties against individuals and businesses that perform regulated work without a license. These penalties can be assessed per violation and can accumulate across multiple instances of unlicensed activity.

**Injunctive relief.** The board or the state can seek a court order prohibiting an unlicensed contractor from continuing to perform regulated work, regardless of pending projects or existing contracts.

**Criminal exposure.** In some cases, willful violation of licensing requirements can rise to the level of a criminal matter, particularly when combined with fraud, misrepresentation, or other unlawful conduct.

**Loss of contract enforceability.** As noted above, a contract for regulated services entered into by an unlicensed contractor may not be fully enforceable, which can affect the contractor's ability to collect payment even for work that was properly performed.

**Reputational damage.** An enforcement action by the NCLCRB is a public matter and can damage the contractor's professional reputation in the market, affecting future business opportunities.

Contractors who are uncertain about whether their services require a license should resolve that uncertainty before performing the work — not after a complaint has been filed.

## What to Do If You've Already Hired an Unlicensed Contractor

If you realize after the fact that the landscape contractor you hired does not hold a valid NC license, your options depend on what has happened with the project:

**If the project has not yet started,** you may be able to cancel the contract or renegotiate. Consult with an attorney licensed in NC before taking any action, particularly if you have already paid a deposit, to understand your rights and the best approach given the specific terms of your contract.

**If the project is underway,** document everything — photograph the work at every stage, save all correspondence, and keep copies of all payments and receipts. Whether to allow the work to continue or stop it depends on the nature and quality of the work and what your contract says. Again, an attorney can help you evaluate your options.

**If the project is complete and you have problems,** your primary recourse is civil court — the NCLCRB cannot help you with a contractor who is not licensed, as the board's authority extends only to its licensees. You may also file a report with the NCLCRB about the unlicensed activity, which can trigger an enforcement investigation even if it does not directly compensate you for your losses.

**In cases involving fraud or theft** — such as a contractor who took your money and did not perform the work — contact local law enforcement and consider filing a complaint with the NC Attorney General's Consumer Protection Division in addition to any civil action.

The best remedy is prevention: always verify a contractor's license before signing a contract or making any payment.
