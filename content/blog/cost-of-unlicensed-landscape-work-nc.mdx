## The Licensing Requirement in NC

North Carolina requires landscape contractors to be licensed through the North Carolina Landscape Contractors' Registration Board (NCLCRB) under Chapter 89D of the General Statutes. The law applies to anyone who performs landscape contracting work for compensation — which covers grading, planting, installation of irrigation systems, hardscape construction, and more.

The licensing requirement exists for a reason. To earn a license, contractors must pass a qualifying exam, demonstrate financial responsibility, and maintain standing with the Board. That process filters out operators who lack the knowledge or accountability to do the work safely and correctly. When a contractor skips it, you lose every protection the system was designed to provide.

## What Can Go Wrong

Consider what happened to a homeowner in the Piedmont who hired an unlicensed crew to install a flagstone patio and a tiered retaining wall along a sloped backyard. The price was about 30 percent below every other bid. The crew worked fast, collected the final payment, and was unreachable within two weeks of finishing.

By the following spring, the retaining wall had shifted. Drainage behind it had nowhere to go. A section collapsed into the yard below, taking a portion of the fence with it. The repairs cost more than the original project. Because the contractor had no license, no registered business address, and had been paid largely in cash, the homeowner had virtually no path to recovery — no bond to claim against, no board to file a complaint with, no paper trail that held up to legal scrutiny.

That scenario is not rare. It is exactly the outcome the licensing system is designed to prevent.

## No Insurance, No Recourse

Licensed landscape contractors in North Carolina are required to carry liability insurance. That coverage protects you if the work damages your property — and it protects you in a more serious situation you may not have considered: worker injury.

If an uninsured worker is hurt on your property, you may face liability as the property owner. Medical costs, lost wages, and legal fees can land on you because the person you hired carried no workers' compensation coverage and no general liability policy. You cannot file a claim against insurance that does not exist. What looked like a bargain at the estimate stage can become a five-figure or six-figure problem that your homeowner's policy may not fully cover.

## Contract Enforceability

North Carolina courts have found, in various contexts, that contracts for services requiring a license may be unenforceable when the provider was unlicensed at the time of the agreement. In practice, this can mean that even if you have a signed contract with an unlicensed landscape contractor, your legal options in civil court are limited. You may not be able to sue for breach of contract in the way you would with a licensed professional. The contractor's unlicensed status, which seemed like an irrelevant background detail, becomes a wall between you and any remedy.

## Permit and Code Issues

Many landscape projects — retaining walls above a certain height, significant grading, irrigation systems tied to municipal water — require permits. Unlicensed contractors routinely skip the permitting process because pulling permits creates a paper record and invites inspection.

If unpermitted work is later discovered, the obligation to fix it falls on the property owner. Municipalities can require you to remediate or even demolish non-compliant work at your expense. If you sell the home, unpermitted improvements can derail the transaction or force you to disclose defects that reduce your asking price. The unlicensed contractor is long gone. The permit problem stays with the property.

## The Risk for Unlicensed Contractors

This is also worth understanding if you are operating without a license, or considering it. Chapter 89D gives the NCLCRB the authority to pursue civil penalties and injunctions against unlicensed operators. Violations can also carry misdemeanor exposure under North Carolina law. The Board actively investigates complaints, and the penalties are not token fines. ## How Homeowners Can Protect Themselves

Before signing any contract for landscape work, take four concrete steps:

- **Verify the license.** Use the NCLCRB's [license verification](/nc-landscape-contractor-license-verification/) tool to confirm the contractor is currently registered and in good standing.
- **Ask for a certificate of insurance.** Request proof of both general liability and workers' compensation coverage, and make sure the policy is current — not expired.
- **Get a written contract.** A signed agreement that specifies the scope of work, materials, timeline, and payment schedule gives you documentation if anything goes wrong.
- **Check references.** Ask for contacts from recent, comparable projects and follow through. An unlicensed operator is unlikely to have a verifiable track record of completed work.

The few minutes that verification takes is the cheapest insurance you can buy — because once the work is done and the crew has moved on, your options narrow fast.
